After graduating from university in May of 2026proud of the spin move, less proud of my GPA..., I went on a motorcycle camping trip. With no set destination, just an urge to leave the place I was starting from, I rode 7,500 miles over 90 days across the US and Canada.

I want to put down at the outset that this was not a trip I undertook, it was one that overtook me. It felt as if an external hand had tidied up the life I had been building for the past four years, put it into a moving box to be left with my roommate, plopped me down on a funny red motorcycle, and pushed me out West.

The sacrifices didn't feel like mine to weigh, the hours not mine to give to anything else. There is no discernible point where this went from idea to impending reality. I think now it was always going to happen. I digress, here's what did.

Beyond the idea of adventure

There are a lot of things one can know in their head yet not feel in their heart. Known only to my head on the morning of June 10th, 2026, was that this would be very difficult.outside my apartment in Washington, D.C. right before setting off. A few points to illustrate what I mean: 1) you need a big motorcycle to go long distances on and off-road. Loaded and fueled, my 20-year-old bike was well over 500 pounds.just about all my gear/supplies laid out before being loaded onto the bike. When I test-rode it fresh out of the motorcycle safety/license course, I couldn't stop the bike without dropping it.the state of my riding skills at the end of April. The previous owner had to ride it back to my apartment's parking garage for me (thanks and sorry, Reid). 2) I had camped fewer than half a dozen times in my life, never backpacked, nor road-tripped or otherwise traveled by myself. This fact nicely dovetailed with 3) there would be no money for hotels (and eventually, even for campsites).

As anybody with a fully developed prefrontal cortex could have guessed: things went very poorly. Within the first third of the trip I: got stranded in a very scary thunderstorm in the woods of Pennsylvania, ran off the road and crashed in Southern NYin my defense, it was a rather sharp turn. (thank you Smiley)Smiley helping me straighten my now-bent handlebars., had my suspension lose all fluid, stranding me in Winnipeg, Manitoba (thank you Brydon, Alyssa, and James Kreutz)JK's suspension shop in the garage behind his house., had my bear spray combust and get in my eyes outside a trailer park in I don't remember where, had my tent half destroyed by a freak hailstorm in Swift Current, and ran out of gas en route to the US borderthis actually worked!. For some reason I thought that this trip would be quiet moments by mountain lakes and driving through slow, wide sunsets. No, it was battle!

Beyond the struggle

And then, all very quickly, it wasn't. It no longer took me an hour to get camp torn down and the bike packed up in the mornings. I was no longer terrified of the hairpins going up and down mountain passes. I got very good at figuring out which of a rural town's three restaurants would have a vegan option (answer: the Chinese one). Things stopped going wrong (or well, stopped going really wrong). And this was all actually kind of sad. It was through my fumbling and ineptitude that I experienced what people will do for others who need help. But beyond that cliché, my newfound competency booted me out of survival mode. And with that, old questions of what I want to do with my life and what is required of me all started to slowly creep back in, stale and as impenetrable as they were before.

The hope that a trip like this would speak clear answers to these old questions has gone unrealized. The act of recounting my experience to myself and others, combined with time, has calcified the original nature of what occurred. What could be put more concretely floated away when I stopped moving, and I am left with a few measly impressions, barely any lessons, and certainly no revelations. See if any of this clicks, though: Something in the way the joy of passing another "Welcome to..." sign mingles with the sight of roadkill. The way you can feel a bear through the forest floor before you hear it sniffing by your tent. How much easier it is to listen to others after days of talking only to yourself. The complete lack of anxiety when dealing with actual danger. A landscape's soul slipping out the back door as soon as you pull out your camera...

Now, on the other side of the country, I ask myself: But have you not gotten what you wanted? The freedom to choose what of your old life to keep, and what to discard? The freedom to go in whichever direction you now feel called to? No, this is hubris. Because at the bottom of this surface-level liberation is the assumption that I would have it 'figured out' by the time I ran out of road. But there is no intellectual understanding that, once reached, we can act from without doubt or difficulty. Now more than ever, I don't know what it is I am called to, if there is an 'I' to be called, or a calling to go towards. But I have resolved to be of service to all I share this planet with while I try to accept the unresolvable. And likely, that process is the end in itself. Thanks for reading.
